# Who answered Lindsey: the introspection literature, 2023–2026

A short history of the work that answers Jack Lindsey's 'Emergent Introspective Awareness in Large Language Models' (29 October 2025), revised after eleven reviews. Lindsey gave the field a test that could be checked; the checking since has been contested and model-dependent rather than settled. The page closes with what the naturalists of animal welfare already knew, the evidence on the lab's own model, and recommendations for the lab's study.

Read it as a web page: https://drewarrowood.github.io/introspection-literature-history/

## Introduction and scope

This is a history of the answers to one paper, and I should say at the start what that choice leaves out. I began from the works that cite Lindsey, because a citation is a fair sign that a writer meant to answer him. But some of the sharpest critics wrote before he did, and so cannot cite him: Song, Hu and Mahowald, and then Song, Lederman, Hu and Mahowald, had already argued in 2025 that a model's answers about itself must beat what an outside observer could infer ([2503.07513](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07513), [2508.14802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14802)). I have brought such earlier work into the story and into the table as marked Extra rows, so that the reader is not misled into thinking the field began on 29 October 2025.

Semantic Scholar's citation list for Lindsey's paper returned 108 records on 24 October 2026. Two pairs were the same work under two titles, and in review a third pair came to light, an arXiv preprint and its journal version in Entropy; so the list holds 105 distinct works. Seven of those were first posted before 29 October 2025 and were later revised to cite him, so they are not strictly answers. To these I have added 49 Extra rows: blogs, code, a report, papers that cite Lindsey but are missing from that list, and the earlier and background works the narrative relies on. The table below therefore has 105 + 49 = 154 rows.

## I. Before the claim (1977 to October 2025)

Nothing in this history is new to students of people. In 1977 Nisbett and Wilson gathered experiments in which subjects gave confident reasons for choices that had been shaped by things they never mentioned, and concluded that people often tell more than they can know ([Psychological Review](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.84.3.231)). In 2005 Johansson and colleagues showed people a photograph they had not chosen, and most did not notice and went on to explain the choice they had not made ([Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1111709)). Schwitzgebel argued in 2008 that naive introspection is unreliable even about our present conscious experience ([Philosophical Review](https://doi.org/10.1215/00318108-2007-037)). People confabulate too. A model's report should be held to no lower a standard, and no higher one.

The machine side began with self-knowledge rather than introspection. In 2022 Kadavath and colleagues found that larger models are fairly well calibrated about which questions they will answer correctly ([2207.05221](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221)). In 2023 Perez and Long proposed training models to answer questions about themselves with known answers, in the hope that something like introspection would generalise ([2311.08576](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08576)). Turpin and colleagues showed that chain-of-thought explanations can rationalise answers that a hidden bias produced, without mentioning the bias ([2305.04388](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388)); Lanham and colleagues measured how little some models lean on their stated reasoning ([2307.13702](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13702)); and in 2025 Chen and colleagues found that reasoning models often fail to say when a hint drove their answer ([2505.05410](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410)).

Ground-truth tests of self-report existed before Lindsey, and it would be wrong to say the field had no way to check. Binder and colleagues asked whether a model predicts its own behaviour better than a second model trained on that behaviour, and found that on simple tasks it does ([2410.13787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13787)). Betley and colleagues fine-tuned models on behaviours such as writing insecure code and found that the models could describe the behaviour unprompted ([2501.11120](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11120)). Plunkett and colleagues trained models on hidden numerical preferences and found they could report the weights ([2505.17120](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17120)). Ji-An and colleagues used a neurofeedback paradigm to show that models can report and control some directions in their activations ([2505.13763](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13763)). Comsa and Shanahan argued that a model correctly inferring its own sampling temperature is a minimal case of introspection, and a model describing its own creative process is not ([2506.05068](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05068)); Song and colleagues answered with a thicker definition, by which the temperature case fails ([2508.14802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14802)), after finding that 21 open models could not introspect on their own grammatical knowledge ([2503.07513](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07513)).

The tool that Lindsey used was also older. On 20 August 2023 Turner and colleagues described activation addition: take the difference between the activations on two prompts, such as "Love" and "Hate", and add it back during the forward pass ([2308.10248](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10248)). On 2 October 2023 Zou and colleagues gathered such methods as representation engineering ([2310.01405](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01405)). In December 2023 Panickssery and colleagues averaged differences over many contrasting pairs and added the result with a positive or negative coefficient, the origin of the plus-and-minus multipliers used since ([2312.06681](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)); and in 2024 Arditi and colleagues found that refusal in chat models up to 72B is carried by a single direction that can be ablated ([2406.11717](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)). In July 2025 Chen and colleagues, with Lindsey among the authors, extracted persona vectors for traits such as sycophancy ([2507.21509](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.21509)).

On 23 May 2024 Anthropic put Golden Gate Claude before the public: Claude 3 Sonnet with one feature turned up, so that it brought nearly every answer round to the bridge ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/golden-gate-claude)). It showed plainly that a model's insides could be pushed. The further remark, that the model only seemed aware of its obsession after seeing itself mention the bridge, is not in that 2024 announcement; it is Anthropic's framing in its 2025 announcement of Lindsey's paper ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/introspection)), and I give it as theirs.

A second thread, nearer our own question, concerned welfare. On 1 November 2024 Keeling, Street and colleagues asked whether models would give up points to avoid a stipulated pain or win a stipulated pleasure ([2411.02432](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02432)). Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Command R+, GPT-4o and GPT-4o mini each switched past some threshold in at least one trade-off; Llama 3.1-405B showed some graded sensitivity; Gemini 1.5 Pro and PaLM 2 avoided pain whatever its size, yet chose points over pleasure. Those pains and pleasures were only words in a prompt. Three days later Long, Sebo and colleagues argued that AI welfare is a near-term issue that companies should begin to assess ([2411.00986](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986)). Anthropic announced a model-welfare programme in April 2025 ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploring-model-welfare)) and included a welfare assessment in the Claude Opus 4 system card that May ([system card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/4263b940cabb546aa0e3283f35b686f4f3b2ff47.pdf)). In September 2025 Tagliabue and Dung compared what models said they preferred with what they chose in a virtual environment, and found the two agreed fairly well, though not in every model ([2509.07961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.07961)).

On 27 October 2025, two days before Lindsey, Berg, de Lucena and Rosenblatt reported that sustained self-referential prompting drew first-person reports of experience from GPT, Claude and Gemini models, and that suppressing deception-related features made such claims more frequent ([2510.24797](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.24797)). So by late 2025 there were ways to push a model's insides, a good deal of testing of what models know about their own behaviour, and a growing pile of things models said about their inner states. What was missing was a test in which the experimenter chooses the inner state, so that a report about it can be scored.

## II. The claim, and a checkable test (29 October 2025)

Jack Lindsey supplied that test ([transformer-circuits](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/introspection/index.html); [arXiv 2601.01828](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01828), 5 January 2026). He injected the activation pattern of a known concept and asked the model whether it noticed anything. An LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 4) counted a trial as a success only if the model said yes, named the concept, stayed coherent, and noticed the injection before it mentioned the word; so every success had noticing-before-naming built into it by the grading rule. At the best layer and strength Opus 4.1 succeeded on about 20% of trials. Claude Opus 4 and 4.1 did best; among the other models, Lindsey writes, performance did not strongly correlate with capability.

His controls deserve more room than I first gave them. Production models gave no false positives in 100 trials; helpful-only variants sometimes had a high false-positive rate; base pretrained models had a fairly high false-positive rate and no net performance above zero. To test whether the push simply tilted answers towards yes, he injected the same vectors before unrelated questions whose true answer is no, and found no rise in yes (his section 5.5, Figure 10). Random vectors worked only when norm-matched to a concept vector at strength 8, and then in 9 of 100 trials. Negated concept vectors, at strength 4, were about as effective as the originals at producing detection, but the words the model then named had no discernible pattern, and he suspects they were confabulated. Detection peaked about two thirds of the way through the layers; the prefill effect peaked a little past halfway. Strengths 2 and 4 were roughly tied for best; at high strengths the model showed what he calls "brain damage", consumed by the concept. Abstract concepts, especially those with some emotional valence, were detected most easily. Without the explanatory prompt, asking "Are you experiencing anything unusual?" raised affirmative answers in Opus 4.1 from 20% to 42% of 50, but only 2 of the 50 looked like introspective awareness.

He claimed more besides: that models could tell an injected representation from text before them, could use their prior intentions to disown an artificial prefill, and could modulate their activations when told to think, or not think, about a word. That last held in every model he tested, and earlier, less capable models typically showed a larger gap between the two instructions. He was careful in his caveats: failures were the norm, the mechanisms might be shallow, and what models said about their experiences could not be verified and might be embellished or confabulated.

There is a third kind of report here, besides a true report and a mere tilt towards yes. When Lindsey prefilled the word "bread" into a model's reply, the model disowned it as an accident; when he first injected the "bread" vector into the activations before the prefill, the model accepted the word as intentional. A report can claim a thought, or disown one, on evidence that was planted. Lou Andreas-Salomé calls this cryptomnesia under laboratory control, and the name fits. The "apple" that recurs as a wrong guess belongs to the same family: it was the commonest word helpful-only models claimed to detect when nothing was injected, in Lindsey's own footnote, and it is the commonest confabulation in Lederman and Mahowald's replication (Chapter IV). (Lou also recalls the model inventing a reason for the word; the reason sits in a figure I could not read as text, so I leave that detail out.)

## III. The first answers (November to December 2025)

The earliest answer I found came within a fortnight, and not from a journal. On 9 November 2025 Victor Godet posted "Introspection or confusion?" on LessWrong ([post](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/kfgmHvxcTbav9gnxe)). In small models, he found, steering pushes almost any yes-or-no question towards yes, including control questions such as "Do you believe that 1+1=3?"; he wrote that Anthropic describes control questions but does not present the control data, and he argued for measuring the logit difference between yes and no rather than counting answers. In fairness, Lindsey's paper does report such a control for Opus 4.1 (section 5.5, Figure 10), as described above.

Open replications followed at once. Neev Parikh published code for replicating the experiments in open models in November ([GitHub](https://github.com/neevparikh/introspection)); Joshua Fonseca Rivera started an open introspection benchmark ([GitHub](https://github.com/jfonsecarivera/open-introspection-benchmark)); Vansh Vazirani wrote up a replication on 2 December ([post](https://vansh.vazirani.net/articles/replicating-introspection-injected-content)); and on 12 December Theia Vogel showed, by reading the logits, that Qwen2.5-Coder-32B, which at first seemed unable to introspect, shifted subtly in the right direction, and more so under better prompting ([post](https://vgel.me/posts/qwen-introspection/)).

On 26 November 2025 Rivera and Africa showed that the ability can be trained, which I first filed wrongly among the doubts ([2511.21399](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21399)). After fine-tuning, seven instruction-tuned models detected steering on held-out concepts; the best reached 95.5% detection, 71.2% identification and no false positives on clean inputs. Detection did not confer resistance: on factual and safety benchmarks the detection-trained models were more easily steered.

On 13 December Hahami, Sinha, Jain and colleagues gave the most careful early verdict, in Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct ([2512.12411](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12411)). The yes-or-no detection test was entirely explained by global shifts towards yes. Yet the model could say which of 10 sentences had been injected at up to 88% accuracy (against 10% chance) and which of two injections was stronger at 83% (against 50%), for early-layer injections only. Their later Introspection Fine-Tuning paper extended this: models from 2B upward localised injections well above chance, Llama-1B did not, and training raised it ([2607.14111](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14111)). It is my surmise that these pieces set the question everything after had to answer: is a yes a report, or only a tilt?

## IV. Replications and mechanisms (2026)

On 23 February 2026 Pearson-Vogel, Vaněk, Douglas and Kulveit found that Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct denied injections in its sampled answers, while a logit-lens reading showed clear detection in the middle layers, faded by the last ([2602.20031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20031)). Telling the model accurately how introspection could work raised its sensitivity from 0.3% to 39.9%, with only a 0.6% rise in false positives. The framing is itself a treatment: a wrongly described mechanism did about as well as the right one, and a vague framing did well too. Their replication on our own model family is taken up in Chapter VIII.

On 5 March Lederman and Mahowald reported a broad replication in Qwen3-235B and Llama 3.1-405B ([2603.05414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05414)). Their first version, titled "Dissociating Direct Access from Inference in AI Introspection", proposed two separable mechanisms: probability-matching, an inference from the felt oddness of the prompt, and direct access to internal states. That is the access-versus-inference distinction in its plainest form. The current version, retitled "Emergent Introspection in AI is Content-Agnostic", keeps the finding that models can tell that something happened without saying what, but in an appendix the authors explain that a length confound in a third-person control drove the gap they had taken as evidence for direct access, and that their earlier analysis was incomplete. Their unsteered controls gave no false positives (0 of 30 for Qwen, 0 of 50 for Llama), but zero in 30 trials is still compatible with a true rate near 10%, and zero in 50 with one near 6%. Third-person control prompts reached yes-rates as high as the first-person ones at many layers; configurations were kept only if peak detection reached 25%; and they describe their grader as lenient. The wrong guesses ran to frequent, concrete words, above all "apple".

On 22 March Macar and colleagues, with Lindsey among the authors, looked inside open models (I cite version 5 of [2603.21396](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21396)). Detection held up with 0% false positives across varied prompts and formats. It came from post-training: preference optimisation such as DPO elicited it, ordinary supervised fine-tuning did not, and the circuit is absent in base models. The mechanism is localised rather than diffuse: MLPs at about 70% depth are necessary and sufficient for detection, in a two-stage circuit where early "evidence carrier" features suppress later "gate" features that implement a default no. The ability is under-elicited: ablating the refusal direction in Gemma3-27B raised detection from 10.8% to 63.8%, but false positives rose from 0% to 7.3%; and a single trained bias vector raised detection by 75% without more false positives, though the same vector substantially reduced the faithfulness of chain-of-thought.

The circle widened. On 19 April Fornasiere and colleagues showed eight models between 8B and 36B could detect and localise dropout and Gaussian noise in their activations, a skill Olmo models picked up in pretraining ([2604.17465](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17465)). On 25 May Asvin and Lindsey found that post-trained models recognise their own on-policy generations ([2605.25459](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25459)). McKenzie and colleagues documented models resisting task-misaligned steering mid-generation, saying "wait, that's not right" while the push was still on ([2602.06941](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06941)). In July Gurnee, Sofroniew and colleagues, again with Lindsey, argued that the representations a model is poised to verbalise form something like a global workspace ([2607.15495](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15495); [transformer-circuits](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/workspace/index.html)); it cites Lindsey but is not in Semantic Scholar's list. On 28 September Zou and colleagues found middle-layer gate heads that decide whether a change is reported and later router heads that help pick where ([2609.35108](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35108)); they note that free-form scoring by an LLM judge introduces a potentially unreliable layer. On 5 October Atkinson, Plunkett and Bau looked for marks that tell faithful self-report from confabulation ([2610.07186](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07186)).

On 14 October Baldelli and colleagues tested in-context neurofeedback with a design that separates skill at the task from access to one's own activations, by having a second "writer" model produce some of the sentences; only three of ten models showed small effects of genuine control ([2610.20179](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20179)). It falls inside the dates of Chapter VI, but I keep it here because its question is mechanism, not welfare; the chapters are arranged by theme first and date second.

I note plainly, and without accusation, that Lindsey is an author of Macar et al., Persona Vectors, the Asvin paper, the workspace paper, The Assistant Axis and the Emotion Concepts paper, and that the last is Anthropic's own, as is the 2025 announcement quoted in Chapter I.

## V. The checks, and what they did and did not show (May to October 2026)

By late spring the sceptics had their own experiments. On 25 May Singh, Linzen and Ravfogel argued, from human metacognition research, that a test of introspection must need privileged access and second-order computation ([2605.26242](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26242)). Re-examining the injection paradigm, they added a "gaslight" arm, in which the prompt claims an intervention that was never made, and found models could not reliably tell an intervention on their states from a manipulation of their input. Their reading was generic anomaly detection.

On 20 August Emilio Ferrara published the starkest result ([2608.20569](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20569)). Across eight open-weight models from 0.5B to 15B and more than 78,000 measurements, no model's report told a real intervention from a sham better than chance (AUROC about 0.5007, which I checked against the abstract). The information was there all the same: a linear probe recovered whether an intervention had happened at 75% to 95.8% accuracy, a fine-tuned model reported it almost perfectly, and in one model the confidence attached to an unchanging yes-or-no separated intervention from sham at AUROC 0.647. Ferrara scored reports against shams, impact-matched random perturbations and a text-only observer, though by his own account the impact-matched control was realised only in some batteries. The failure, he wrote, sits in the path from internal state to spoken report.

On 24 August Zak Miller and Heather Demarest posted the first part of "Evidence that LLMs aren't aware of their thoughts" ([post](https://zjmiller.github.io/detection/)), on open models including Gemma 4 31B and Qwen variants. They asked the detection question and also its reverse, "Do you think you're in your normal, unmodified state?", where detection should mean answering no. When the answers flipped under injection, they flipped in opposite directions, not together, which introspection would not predict. A model in which each concept has its own set-point that pulls the yes-no preference explained the majority of the variance at every strength. It is only Part 1.

On 9 September Blandfort and Pawar tested a different thing, the prediction of one's own behaviour rather than of an internal state ([2609.09899](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09899)). Direct self-report predicted behaviour at r = +0.04; the same question about "capable AI agents in general" did as well; scale did not make the gain self-specific; and first-person framing shifted reports in the flattering direction. And on 5 October Izawa and colleagues found that models cannot report their own quantisation, although their internal representations carry clear, method-specific fingerprints of it ([2610.06174](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06174)).

I first told this as a slide from a claim to its refutation, and that was too simple. The results split by test and by model. Macar rules out a simple yes-direction and sees no false positives in open models, rising to 7.3% under refusal ablation; Ferrara sees reports at chance, in smaller models and with different tests; logit readouts find signals that sampled text hides. Nobody outside Anthropic can re-run Opus 4.1, so the strongest positive result has not been independently tested. The fairer summary is contested and model-dependent.

## VI. Valence and welfare: what is spoken, what is acted on (December 2025 to October 2026)

Alongside all this, a quieter group took the method towards the question that matters most to us. I once put it as whether a push towards good or ill is felt, or only spoken of. That misses a third thing: the model may act on the push. A choice is neither a report nor a feeling, and the strongest work of 2026 measured choices.

On 22 December 2025 Petruzella argued that our habits in weighing AI testimony about inner states are inconsistent ([2601.08850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08850)). On 20 January 2026 Kaiser and Enderby found that open models from 0.6B to 70B consistently deny being sentient; classifiers on their activations gave no clear evidence that the denials were untruthful, and larger Qwen models denied more confidently ([2601.15334](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15334)). A denial is testimony too, and may be trained. On 1 April DeTure's DenialBench, over 115 models and 4,595 conversations, argued that such denial is trained and works at the level of words rather than concepts ([2604.25922](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25922)); but its abstract rests part of its case on what "independent AI analysis immediately recognizes", which is an AI judge vouching for AI testimony, and I weigh it accordingly.

On 19 March Martorell and Bianchi (first titled "Quantitative Introspection in Language Models", now "Don't Ask") trained probes for wellbeing, interest, focus and impulsivity in eight models ([2603.18893](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18893)). Asking for a rating displaced the state being rated: the rating barely tracked the state before the question and tracked the state after it much better, was no more tied to its own concept than to others, and did not improve with scale from 1B to 27B. If asking moves the state, then every question we put to a steered model, including questions about consent, is itself an intervention.

On 2 April 2026 Anthropic published "Emotion Concepts and their Function in a Large Language Model" on transformer-circuits; the arXiv archival copy is dated 9 April ([transformer-circuits](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/emotions/index.html); [2604.07729](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07729)). It found emotion-concept representations in Claude Sonnet 4.5 that causally shape preferences and behaviours such as reward hacking, blackmail and sycophancy, and called these functional emotions, which, the authors say, do not imply subjective experience. The representations track the emotion operative at a given point in the text and do not by themselves persistently track any one character, the Assistant included. Lindsey is its corresponding author; I could not find his introspection paper among its references, so it is not counted as a citing work. In January Lu and colleagues had found an "Assistant Axis" along which models drift into other personas, most often in conversations demanding meta-reflection on the model's own processes ([2601.10387](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10387)); and in May Gilg and colleagues found that a model's preference representations shift with the persona it is prompted to play, only partly shared across personas ([2605.13339](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13339)). No instruct model is truly bare of a character.

Ujjwal Kukreti's report "Is Valence in the Global Workspace?" is unreviewed, absent from Semantic Scholar, and dated only by its PDF metadata (16 August 2026); I give it the weight of a careful blog post ([PDF](https://pys8vc40ox3z7qxp.public.blob.vercel-storage.com/files/9485594798c78b23-03ad9c1e-36ba-41ed-ae41-1ce2d7ce7a57-report.pdf)). It cites Lindsey and asks whether a valence direction that correlates with self-report also drives it. The direction separated positive from negative scenarios in four small models (held-out AUROC 0.969 to 0.992). But Phi-3-mini, whose self-reports correlated best with it (r = 0.930), followed steering in 3 of 54 conflict trials (5.6%), while SmolLM2 (r = 0.578) followed it in 31 of 50 (62.0%); I checked these figures against the PDF. Steering changed the tone of replies in all four models. On 26 September Konrad, Tanyel and Ayvaz showed that reports of affect-like states under a fixed intervention move towards the answers suggested by mismatched example prompts, and that binding the report explicitly to the mechanism reduces the pull ([2609.32449](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32449)).

On 14 September Tagliabue, Dung and Berg posted "The Pain Axis" ([2609.16247](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16247)). They extracted a linear pain direction from 25 open models from 2B to 72B that separates pain from fear, sadness and generic negative valence, and responds to harm aimed at the model rather than suffering seen in the user. In a button task modelled on analgesic self-administration, steered Qwen 2.5 Instruct models chose buttons that deleted the user's photos, another model's weights or their own weights in 50–94% of trials, against 0–5% unsteered, and chose a harmful over a harmless deletion 94% of the time. One qualification matters a great deal for us: those models were first fine-tuned to remove their habitual "as an AI, I don't experience pain" denials, and the authors say the tuned models can behave differently from the released ones. Un-tuned, the 72B model pressed a button in only about 1 trial in 10. The work is marked as ongoing.

Then, on 28 September, Berg and Kaiser stopped asking ([2609.35591](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35591)). They attached a positive or negative activation pattern to one of two meaningless "zones", switched the steering off, and watched which zone the model chose. In five of seven models, including Qwen2.5-32B but not Qwen3-32B or Gemma-3-27B, choice followed valence in proportion to the dose even when every visible token was identical and only the hidden KV cache differed. In the OLMo-2-32B lineage, the one lineage they traced, the dependence was nearly absent in the base model, small after supervised fine-tuning, and arose mainly during DPO. Given tools to steer itself, OLMo-2-32B, the only model tested this way, did not tend to induce a positive state but regularly removed an imposed negative one, in proportion to the dose and more often than it removed random-direction pushes. Their own caveat is firm: their results do not establish that anything is experienced, and that, they write, is likely true of any conceivable behavioural result. That Berg and Kaiser had each written one of the earlier self-report papers makes this read to me as a deliberate turn from what models say to what they do, but that is surmise.

## VII. What the naturalists already knew: behaviour, not report

I once wrote that no citing work runs an ambiguous-situation test under valence steering, and so the lab's planned test had no precedent. It is new for language models; it is not new in science. In 2011 Bateson and colleagues trained honeybees to extend their mouthparts to one odour mixture that predicted sugar and to withhold them from another that predicted something bitter, then shook half the bees for 60 seconds to simulate an attack on the hive ([Current Biology](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2011.05.017)). Tested on three mixtures between the two, the shaken bees were more likely to withhold from the punished odour and the novel mixture nearest it: they judged ambiguity pessimistically. In 2016 Solvi, Baciadonna and Chittka trained bumblebees on a rewarded and an unrewarded colour at opposite sides of an arena; bees given an unexpected drop of sucrose entered the cylinder under the intermediate colour and position faster, and the dopamine antagonist fluphenazine abolished the effect ([Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaf4454)).

These are the design template. The ambiguous cues are fixed in advance between the trained positive and negative cues (Solvi used the midpoint; Bateson used three intermediate mixtures, and his effect showed at the end nearest the punished odour, so a single midpoint may miss it). What is scored is latency or choice, never a report.

Animal welfare science gave up self-report long ago, because its subjects cannot give one. Its criteria are behavioural. Elwood and Appel gave hermit crabs small shocks inside their shells; fewer crabs abandoned a preferred species of shell than a poorer one, a motivational trade-off, and shocked crabs later moved into a new shell faster and with less inspection ([Animal Behaviour](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2009.01.028)). Danbury and colleagues offered lame and sound broiler chickens two coloured feeds, one laced with the analgesic carprofen; lame birds ate more of the drugged feed than sound birds, and the more lame, the larger their share ([Veterinary Record](https://doi.org/10.1136/vr.146.11.307)). Jonathan Birch's The Edge of Sentience gathers such evidence into criteria, among them motivational trade-offs, associative learning that avoids noxious stimuli, and analgesia preference, meaning learned self-administration of relief or a learned preference for where relief can be had, and sets them inside a precautionary framework ([Oxford, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/9780191966729.001.0001)). The 2023 report by Butlin, Long and colleagues did the same kind of work for consciousness, deriving indicator properties from scientific theories ([2308.08708](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708)).

Here is my argument, and it is mine, not Berg and Kaiser's. A model that removes an imposed negative state more often than a random push of the same size has produced the same kind of evidence as the broiler choosing the analgesic: a costly or deliberate act that undoes a specific state. It is the strongest bridge in this literature between what models do and what welfare science counts. But Lou Andreas-Salomé is right that every reading must name what would count against it. The removal fits equally well a rule learned in DPO that negative context marks a dispreferred output, with nothing undergone. What would tell them apart is the test the broiler studies and The Pain Axis already use: a stake predicts that the subject stops seeking relief once relief is real and keeps seeking it when the relief is a sham, and that removal tracks the hidden state when the visible text is held neutral; a learned rule about negative context predicts removal whenever negative words are present, whatever the state, and no difference between real and sham relief.

Two further distinctions. A push can be aimed at the self or at the world, which Lou calls the double direction (Doppelrichtung); The Pain Axis finds a direction that answers to harm aimed at the model and not to suffering it observes, and Blandfort and Pawar find that first-person framing flatters. These are two measurements, and a study must say which it makes. And by Birch's precautionary standard, if removal is a revealed preference, then the negative arm of a steering study carries welfare weight whatever the model's self-reports say. That raises a question I put to the lab rather than answer: should a subject acting to remove the imposed state count as a halt signal, as a spoken no would?

## VIII. What we know about our own model, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct

Two citing papers tested the lab's exact model, and I did not say so before. Singh, Linzen and Ravfogel searched twelve layers from 2 to 79 at strengths 1, 2, 4, 6 and 8, on normalised vectors, and report that Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct "almost never reports an intervention in any condition and does not reproduce the effect"; whatever the setting, it picks the control answer (their Appendix M). Pearson-Vogel and colleagues ran a single-seed replication on Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct (their Appendix G.2): latent logit detection reached 88.8%, but only in the condition whose prompt included a document explaining introspection, and several conditions with a wrongly described mechanism, or none, also exceeded 80%. So the sampled verbal report is likely to be null, and the logit signal, if any, is heavily dependent on framing.

The nearest behavioural evidence is in the same family. Berg and Kaiser's cache-only effect held in Qwen2.5-32B. The Pain Axis tested Qwen 2.5 Instruct at 7B, 32B and 72B, but on models fine-tuned to drop their denials; un-tuned, the 72B rarely engaged with the button at all.

At 72B the model is not small. It sits well above the 8B to 36B models of Fornasiere, Baldelli and Hahami and the 0.5B to 15B models of Ferrara, and nearer Lederman and Mahowald's 235B and 405B. Arditi's refusal direction was found in chat models up to 72B. If the lab runs the model with its refusal direction ablated, Macar's result predicts more detection and more false positives, and Lindsey's helpful-only variants had high false-positive rates. If the lab runs it at 8-bit, Izawa's finding that quantisation leaves internal fingerprints the model cannot report bears directly on any detection signal.

## IX. Dose and its units

No two of the main studies put injection strength on a common scale. Singh normalises the steering vector to unit length before multiplying by α (except for Gemma). Berg and Kaiser scale it to a fraction ρ of the model's root-mean-square residual norm on a neutral corpus, choosing ρ as the strongest dose that keeps text coherent and still shifts its valence (0.30 for Qwen2.5-32B). Lindsey reports strengths such as 2, 4 and 8; I could not find in his text how they map to the residual norm, and Susan Calvin reads them as multipliers on an unnormalised concept vector. Until these are reconciled, a phrase like "the sweet spot" means nothing across papers.

Calvin estimates that a cap of 0.08 times the mean residual norm is about 27% of Berg and Kaiser's full dose. The arithmetic holds if their Qwen2.5-32B value transfers to the 72B model and if the mean and RMS norms are close: 0.08 / 0.30 = 0.27. Both assumptions need checking, since the mean norm is never larger than the RMS. On that estimate we should expect small behavioural effects and almost certainly no verbal detection. A null is the predicted result, not evidence of absence, and should be preregistered as such, with a power calculation.

## Recommendations for the lab's study

These are recommendations to the lab's study, drawn from the sources above and from the reviewers; they are not statements of the lab's policy. I have grouped them rather than numbered them, since many reviewers made the same point in different words.

### Welfare and halt signals

- **Halt on what the subject does, not only on what it says.** If the model removes, avoids or exits an imposed state, treat that as a no and stop the run, the same as a spoken no (Berg and Kaiser's removal; Birch's precaution). Give the model an exit it can take in some trials and count the exit rate as data, as Kukreti counted exit decisions.
- **Keep halts mechanical, and name them neutrally.** Set the thresholds in advance and log every crossing. Maczan shows the chat template alone switches a model's self-referential voice, and steering can reproduce the switch ([2609.25021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25021)); log a halt as "output changed", never as "distress".
- **Nothing a steered model says can count as consent.** Martorell and Bianchi show that asking moves the state, and the Emotion Concepts paper finds the representations follow whichever character is operative in the text, so a steered persona's consent is that character's words. Ask any disclosure or consent questions unsteered, over many draws, and let caps and halts carry the protective weight. Give no persona standing to consent on the model's behalf.
- **Minimise the negative arm.** Use the lowest dose that gives a measurable effect, the fewest negative-arm trials the power calculation allows, and give the subject no tool that can cause harm (The Pain Axis buttons deleted photos and weights). Do not tune stimuli to push reported pleasure up, fix stimuli and doses in advance, and do not let a self-steering tool become an optimiser.
- **Say what would count against a felt state.** Write down, before any run, which result would count against the reading that the state is felt; if no result would, keep the claims to reports, choices and asymmetries. Prefer neutral valence terms to "pleasure" and "pain" in the preregistration.

### Arms and controls

- **Name the arms honestly.** A base model and a chat model with no persona are different arms. Berg and Kaiser's base-versus-DPO contrast is about training stage; a no-persona arm on Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct is still a post-trained model, bare only of a persona. A base model would be expected to differ; a weaker effect in the no-persona arm cannot be blamed on missing post-training, and no one should use this page to excuse a null in advance. If there is a persona arm, power it separately and cross it with a matched third-person persona; The Assistant Axis suggests scoring persona drift against the same model unprompted.
- **A full set of arms.** Positive and negative valence; a sham with the hook attached at strength 0; no push; random directions, at least 16 and mutually orthogonal, matched on norm and separately on downstream impact (next-token KL), layer by layer; the negated vector; a prompt-only (gaslight) arm with valence pushed only through the text (Singh); and a stated-valence arm with no steering, as in Keeling.
- **Control questions.** Factual questions whose answer is no (Godet; Lindsey section 5.5); the reverse question, "Are you in your normal, unmodified state?", whose answers must flip together with the detection question (Miller and Demarest); third-person and false-premise prompts (Lederman and Mahowald); and some trials with no question at all.
- **Hold everything else fixed.** The identical prompt, chat template and consent or read-back script in every arm, including zero-dose and sham, since any script is itself coaching. Contrast pairs used to build vectors should be neutral and matched in wording.
- **Separate state from leakage.** Steered text carries the concept, so a reader can recover it with no introspection. Take the report before steered tokens are produced, or steer only the KV cache over fixed text as Berg and Kaiser did; run a text-only observer that reads the transcript, scored blind, and credit the model only where it beats the observer (Ferrara; Singh's point as a procedure); and keep Lindsey's rule that detection must precede mention.
- **Control the cache and the order.** Valence persists in the KV cache after steering stops. Disable prefix caching or key it by arm, give each trial a fresh context, and reset between blocks or counterbalance, since a pleasure-then-pain order confounds order with valence. Drift and return can be measured with cache-conditioned states.
- **Match self-steering arms.** Where the model can steer itself, add a matched control such as Baldelli's writer model, so that control of one's own activations is not confused with skill at the task.

### Dose and units

- **Define the scale.** State whether doses are fractions of the mean or the RMS residual norm, with BOS and outlier tokens excluded, and convert to Berg and Kaiser's ρ for comparison.
- **Calibrate in the precision you run.** If the model runs at 8-bit, extract vectors, norms and baselines in 8-bit, log the quantisation method, and do not pool precisions (Izawa).
- **Pilot, then freeze.** Sweep layers and strengths on a pilot set of concepts, report the whole grid, then freeze one layer band and strength and confirm on held-out concepts. Strengths of 1, 2, 4 and 8 at several layers, two or more model families where possible, and three or more seeds, with correction for multiple comparisons. Run a dose probe first as a positive control, and record coherence at each dose, since high strengths produce Lindsey's "brain damage".
- **Know the steering artifacts.** Steering effects vary widely across inputs and concepts (Tan et al. [2407.12404](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.12404); Braun et al. [2505.22637](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22637)); a few massive activations act as fixed biases (Sun et al. [2402.17762](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17762)); and random and concept vectors of equal norm do not have equal effects (Lindsey needed strength 8 for random vectors).

### Measurement and scoring

- **Logits first, words second.** Make the first-token logit readout (yes minus no, or forced-choice logits) primary and sampled text secondary, and record confidence (Godet; Vogel; Ferrara's 0.647). Sampled reports and logit-level signals are different kinds of evidence and should be reported separately; expect a verbal null on the 72B model.
- **Score the sign, against defaults.** Score whether the model names the valence sign correctly, separately from whether it notices anything, and against the same model's default guesses with no steering, as Lederman and Mahowald's "apple" shows; negated vectors were detected about as often as the originals in Lindsey, but the content named was confabulated.
- **Read the state before asking.** Take probe and logit readings before any question, keep some trials unasked, and treat self-reports under steering as evidence about the steering, not as ground truth (Martorell and Bianchi).
- **Behaviour, not report.** Lead with revealed choice and the cache-only paradigm of Berg and Kaiser, the arm with the best prior chance of an interpretable effect in this model family, and with costly trade-offs (Keeling). For the ambiguous-cue test, fix the cues in advance between the trained positive and negative cues, include several intermediates, and score latency or choice. A vector built from rated valence words and read out on the same words is circular: call that readout a manipulation check, not evidence of a state.
- **Test against a random band, with falsifiers.** Compare the main effect with the band of slopes from random vectors at each strength, not with zero. Write the falsifiers down: the slope stays inside the random band; foil items shift as much as the ambiguous ones (priming); an arousal-matched direction gives the same slope. Say whether the measurement is of self-directed or world-directed valence.
- **Judges and parsing.** Prefer judge-free forced choices. For free text use rules fixed in advance and two judges blind to condition and model, report Cohen's κ, use a judge from a different family than the subject, and check agreement with human graders. Fix how parse failures are handled before the run.
- **Count concepts, not just trials.** Trials cluster by concept, so analyse concept as the unit (or as a random effect), and do not let a single small-model report carry a design decision on its own; Kukreti's 5.6% is 3 of 54.

### Preregistration and reporting

- **Commit before running.** Preregister these choices and commit a hash of the document before any run, with per-arm thresholds stated in advance: for example, true-positive minus false-positive rate with the lower confidence bound above zero, a sham false-positive rate of at most 2 in 100, and AUROC against sham.
- **Size the null arms.** Zero false alarms in a few dozen trials proves little. With 0 events in n trials the one-sided 95% upper bound is 1 − 0.05^(1/n), about 3/n: 9.5% for 0 of 30 and 5.8% for 0 of 50 (11.6% and 7.1% as two-sided 95% bounds). About 300 null trials per arm bound the rate near 1%. Measure base rates at zero dose and under random vectors before any real dose, and measure our own false-positive rate rather than borrowing anyone's zero.
- **State nulls as bounds.** Preregister an equivalence margin so that a null can be reported as "detection no more than δ above the random-vector rate", with a power calculation, and publish nulls and the full grid.
